Inside the decision

Each candidate carries two independent scores: how confident the few-shot detector is that something is there, and how confident the verifier is that it is the right species. Plotting them against each other shows which stage is doing the work.

Stage 01

Learn the sound

Five calls become a compact fingerprint, from a network trained on 40 other Indian species — never the target.

Stage 02

Adapt to the recording

Wind, insects, distance and time of day change everything. The model briefly retunes itself to each recording before listening.

Stage 03

Verify

A large pretrained bird model re-checks every candidate, cutting false alarms from roughly six per ten true hits down to two.

05

The finding

Verification does not just help the hardest case — it helps every species we tested, and the size of the effect is what makes it a finding rather than an anecdote.

Species discrimination · before and after verification, all 10 species

0.50 is the coin-flip line: the detector cannot tell the target from an acoustically similar decoy at all. Verification clears it for every species, by a margin larger than the measured noise floor in all ten cases.

Control

Matching alone is inconsistent — and its worst case is our own species

We plant the target and four acoustically similar decoy species into the same recordings at the same loudness, then ask a threshold-free question: does the target score above the decoy? Stage 1 alone spans 0.464 to 0.701 across ten species — one sits below chance, the rest above it, some barely. The lowest score in the set is Sholicola albiventris — IUCN Near Threatened (2024 assessment), High Conservation Concern per State of India's Birds — the species this project was built around, and not a randomly chosen case. We say that plainly rather than let it be found.

Fix

Verification lifts every single one

Passing every stage-1 candidate to a Perch-embedding verifier lifts mean species AUC to 0.749 — a gain of +0.161 (SE 0.020, d/SE 8.13), and every one of the ten gains clears the encoder-training noise floor on its own. False alarms on bird-free audio fall sharply throughout — as much as 72.5 → 1.5 per hour for the easiest species. This has now been run three independent times — gains of +0.165, +0.166, +0.161 — with the largest per-species move between any two sessions at 0.009.

A number we retired, and why — and an honest coincidence worth addressing directly. An earlier build reported stage-1 at 0.459 for this species — below chance. That figure came from a superseded pipeline (Perch v1, missing input normalisation, a leak that let support recordings into the held-out pool) and was retired. Since then we've measured this species' stage-1 AUC six separate times on the corrected pipeline: 0.517 and 0.509 in earlier smaller runs, and 0.469, 0.470, 0.464 across three independent full 10-species runs (0.456–0.480 across encoder seeds within any one of them). Those three figures sit close to the retired number — by coincidence, not by relapse: decoys are ranked among the species NOT chosen as targets, so a 10-target run and a 2-target run draw this species' decoys from different pools. Here the peak decoy similarity is actually lower than in the earlier runs (0.419 vs 0.575) yet the score came out harder — a reminder that one similarity number doesn't capture the full difficulty of a decoy set. Read whbsho3's honest range across every correct-pipeline measurement as roughly 0.46–0.52, not a single point estimate.
06

Does it generalise?

A result on one bird is an anecdote. The full pre-registered run — ten target species, each with its own five examples, its own four hardest decoys, and an encoder that has never seen it — is what turns it into a finding.

Per-species · stage 1 vs verified
Outcome A — it generalises, properly earned this time. Verification helped 10 of 10 species; none fell below chance after verification. Mean gain +0.161 (SE 0.020, d/SE 8.13) — a far tighter result than the 2-species smoke test that came before it (d/SE 2.77). Every individual species' gain clears the measured encoder-training noise floor on its own, so this isn't a handful of species riding on a few noisy outliers.
What we learned about difficulty. Nine species read this analysis before us and found no consistent predictor of task difficulty (Nolasco et al. 2023, §4.1). Across our ten species, call stereotypy — how consistent a species' calls are with each other — correlates with stage-1 AUC at r=0.59 and with the verification gain at r=−0.56: species with more variable calls score worse before verification and gain more from it. Directional, not proof (n=10, below the significance threshold at this sample size) — but it's mechanistically sensible, and it's exactly the kind of factor the field has been looking for. Sholicola albiventris, this project's founding species, has the lowest stereotypy in the set and the lowest stage-1 score. That's not a coincidence we're hiding from either.
One limitation worth stating plainly. Nine of the ten species have 500 available recordings; the pre-registration rule selects by recording count, and the tenth — the actual rare, data-poor endemic this project is about — is the outlier by necessity. The result generalises across common species with strong data; whbsho3 remains the project's one genuine proof point for the hard case the motivation is actually about.
Does the advantage survive fainter calls?

Every specificity number above was measured at a fixed +6 dB — loud, close calls. A pre-registered prediction, made before this ever ran: the verifier's advantage should shrink as calls get fainter.

Raw AUC falls because _auc scores an unlocalised call 0.0 — so a missed detection and a real species mix-up look identical to that metric. Excluding calls stage 1 never found at all tells a different story: AUC among localised calls is 0.849 → 0.849 — a change of exactly zero — from +9 dB down to −6 dB. What degrades with SNR is coverage, 86% down to 60%, not species discrimination.

The pre-registered prediction was wrong, and the actual result is the stronger one. Species discrimination does not shrink as calls get fainter — it is flat within noise across the whole tested range, once a call is actually found. What fails in quiet audio is detection, not identification. A bug in our own first pass at this analysis briefly buried the result: a verdict check tested only whether a change was distinguishable from noise, not which side of zero it landed on — so a change of exactly 0.000 printed as "too close to call" instead of the flat result it actually was. Caught against the real numbers same session, fixed, and covered with a regression test asserting the old rule really did misclassify this case.
07

Choosing the configuration

Every knob below was measured rather than assumed — including the ones where the published literature turned out not to transfer. Switch the metric to see how each choice trades off.

How many examples do you actually need?

The premise of the whole project is "five". This tests it.

One shot is clearly not enough; beyond three, we honestly can't resolve it. 1→3 buys +0.067 — a real step. 3→5 buys +0.042 (right at the edge of the noise floor) and 5→10 buys +0.027 (inside it), against a 0.043 run-to-run spread measured at this sample size (3 species) — now measured a second independent time, with results this close to the first pass. We're not claiming a plateau we can't back up — only that going from one example to a handful clearly matters, and beyond that this study can't distinguish the steps from noise yet.

Which five examples?

Nobody has published this for few-shot bioacoustics. Our honest answer: it isn't resolved yet either.

Spread across strategies (0.073, second independent measurement — was 0.068 the first time) is smaller than the run-to-run noise at this sample size (0.024 mean sd) — and the per-species winners disagree completely: rated wins for barswa, random for bcnher, diverse for whbsho3. Only "first five, no curation" (the DCASE default) looks consistently weaker. We're not claiming a best strategy — we're reporting that it isn't settled, honestly, rather than picking the number that looks best.

Published techniques, tested here

Each bar is one configuration. The dashed line is our baseline; the grey band is the measured noise floor — anything inside it is not a real effect.

Complete 8-configuration factorial on a 3-species subset (§06 above is the full 10-species confirmation). An earlier attempt at this same design was killed by a quota cutoff at 3 of 10 planned runs — checkpointing meant nothing already-measured was lost, and a second session finished the remaining 7 without re-paying for the first 3. The ranking of techniques below is expected to hold at full species scale since it's about architecture choices, not species coverage — that remains an assumption, not re-verified, but the design itself is no longer partial. The baseline is repeated across three encoder seeds to establish the noise floor (spread 0.009 in this run — the tightest measured yet); every other configuration is a single run. TimeFilterAug was dropped from this design after an earlier run measured it harmful in isolation.

Every published technique we imported made it worse. Four methods — two from DCASE-winning systems, one from a peer-reviewed paper, one from Google's own Perch 2.0 release — were each tested against a measured noise floor. All four reduced species AUC, and the untouched baseline came out ahead of every one of them. We report this as the result it is: in a five-shot regime with a 250-step fine-tune, techniques tuned for full-scale training budgets did not transfer.
Contradicts the literature

PCEN beats log-Mel here

Switching front-end costs −0.107 AUC as a main effect (d/SE −3.44, complete factorial), and log-Mel loses in every paired configuration across three independent runs. The DCASE 2024 winner reported the opposite on F1 — but the same literature shows PCEN winning on precision, and species discrimination is a precision-shaped problem. The right front-end depends on which question you are asking.

Did not transfer

The prototypical probe — now clearly rejected, not just borderline

Perch 2.0's own paper reports prototypical probing beating a linear probe by +0.078 mAP. Here it cost −0.048 AUC (d/SE −5.35 on the complete factorial — this cleared the noise floor outright; an earlier partial run had it sitting right at the floor, borderline). Event F1 and false alarms make the case just as clearly: FA/hr rose 8.2 → 27.8 on PCEN and 3.2 → 37.0 on log-Mel. Three metrics, both front-ends, same direction. Rejected.

08

How we know what's real

The most useful measurement in this project was an accident. It decides which of the results above we are allowed to believe.

The accident

The same experiment, twice

One configuration was run identically in two separate sessions — same species, same seeds, same code. It scored 0.696 once and 0.731 the next time. Nothing differed but GPU scheduling: convolution backward passes are not bit-reproducible even with a fixed random seed.

Retraining the encoder on an identical bank moves results by 0.025; across sessions the gap reaches 0.035. Both are now measured directly rather than assumed.

The consequence

A verdict was withdrawn, then re-earned

Before the floor was known, the pipeline called the prototypical probe harmful on a d/SE = −3.02 — a standard error that only measured variation within one run. The effect it was judging, −0.005, was seven times smaller than the floor. That verdict was retracted.

A later, properly-ordered run measured the probe again at −0.034 on a partial factorial — sitting right at the floor, borderline — then −0.048 (d/SE −5.35) once the full 8-config design finished. Rejected on false-alarm rate throughout, regardless of where the AUC number landed. Same conclusion, arrived at honestly three times.

Every measured effect against the noise floor

Bars inside the grey band are not distinguishable from run-to-run randomness, regardless of how confident the surrounding statistics look.

Why this is in the write-up at all. It would have been easy to report the prototypical probe as a clean negative result and move on. Measuring the floor first is what separates a real effect from an artefact of the hardware — and it forced us to withdraw a conclusion we had already drawn.
